In an interview accompanying the DVD release of _Radio On_, director/writer Chris Petit uses the word ‘autistic’ on three separate occasions to describe the mood he was aiming for. Embarking on his debut screenplay while still _Time Out_’s film editor, Petit was less concerned with plot or character, dialogue or drama, than with ‘buildings and weather’. The minimal narrative – a man, Robert B. (David Beames), drives from London to Bristol to investigate his brother’s mysterious death – is a mere ploy to introduce the principal character: the urban landscape of late 1970s Britain. Somewhere between Martin Parr’s _Boring Postcards_ and Anton Corbijn’s _Control_, the movie has, as Petit notes, a documentary value first if not foremost. These clapped-out vistas of service stations, flyovers, industrial estates and business hotels rarely made it into UK cinemas of the period. _Radio On_ is not picturesque, then, but it is superlatively filmic, thanks to Martin Schäfer’s cinematography, so high-contrast that darkness often swallows up the screen, while during night scenes electric lighting burns through the murk with magnesium ferocity.

_Radio On_ was an Anglo-German production, jointly funded by the BFI and Wim Wenders’ Road Movies company. Interviewing the German director for _Time Out_, Petit had plucked up the courage to say, ‘Actually, I’ve got this script.’ Amazingly, his hero not only part-funded _Radio On_, he also loaned Petit his cinematographer Schäfer, and his then wife Lisa Kreuzer took the female lead. The influence of Wenders’ road trilogy is strong, but equally important is another crucial mid-1970s trilogy: J.G. Ballard’s _Crash_/_High-Rise_/_Concrete Island_. The latter, a short novel about a modern-day castaway trapped on scrubland beneath the Westway flyover after being injured in a car crash, leaps to mind during the long aerial shot of Robert driving out of London, the camera lingering lovingly on the gash-ugly industrial wasteland that surrounds the flyover. Another literary reference point is Margaret Drabble’s _The Ice Age_, a novel often aligned with punk as a document of the malaise and bunkered-down anomie of mid-1970s Britain. Filmed in the month of January, _Radio On_ exudes a coldness both physical and emotional. One song on the marvellously apt and evocative soundtrack sings plaintively about being ‘free from the frozen years at last’.

Petit uses pop songs to externalise the feelings that his taciturn characters can’t or won’t express. That’s not especially radical in itself, but music is deployed in an unconventional way. A tune, ostensibly played on the in-car stereo, might abruptly dip down in volume but continue to play without fading away; Devo's stilted subversion of ‘(I Can’t Get No) Satisfaction' is made even more disjointed and alienated by being chopped into shards. Petit says he consciously reversed cinema’s vision-over-sound hierarchy, aiming to make ‘sound with pictures’. You could almost imagine _Radio On_ as a series of promo videos responding to the potency of the era’s vanguard pop.

_Radio On_’s title itself comes from the Modern Lovers’ proto-punk anthem ‘Roadrunner’, a hymn to the American dream of perpetual mobility. Jonathan Richman sings ‘I’m in love with the modern world,’ but _Radio On_’s vision of modernity is unlovely and loveless. Robert’s motto could be ‘only disconnect’. There’s a series of brittle interactions with aggrieved-seeming English women, from Robert’s live-in girlfriend who moves out without him even noticing, to the dead brother’s lover (who bears a striking resemblance to post-punk ice queen Siouxsie Sioux). Even doing his job as an all-night DJ at a factory, Robert lacks empathy. Reading out a female worker’s request for ‘Help Me Make It through the Night,’ he says ‘Here’s something better’ and plays Ian Dury’s ‘Sweet Gene Vincent’ instead.

_Radio On_’s permafrost thaws slightly only a couple of times. Intimacy of a sort develops between Robert and Ingrid, a German woman (Kreuzer) searching for her daughter. The other moment occurs at a derelict petrol station, where Robert meets a rockabilly-obsessed singer (a cameo from the then not-very-famous Sting) and the two men duet on ‘Three Steps to Heaven’ (originally by Eddie Cochran, who died in a car crash on the nearby A4 – how Ballardian is that?). But these are ships-that-pass encounters. ‘Last night I hoped we would sleep together,’ says Ingrid. ‘But we won’t.’

In _Radio On_, the car is an ambiguous symbol, promising not the freedom of the open road but the emptiness of a life unmoored from emotional ties. The road movie, as Petit knew from the off, wouldn’t translate to the UK’s spaghetti junctions and bypasses, but that was the point. Just compare the word ‘motorway’ – as dourly, phlegmatically British as ‘mustn’t grumble’ – with ‘highway’ and ‘freeway’: it simply doesn’t set off the same utopian resonances of liberation.

_Radio On_ shies away from a political reading – Petit was adamantly opposed to social realism – but political reality leaks into Robert’s bubbleworld nonetheless, as the radio drip-drips endless bad news from the IRA and Baader-Meinhof, reports of heavy snow paralysing the country… Society is falling apart but Robert is all uncomprehending detachment: as he tells a hitchhiker, ‘I never understood what the problems were.’ At one point he strolls past a wall advertising shock absorbers. Cars have them, but they can also be them, insulating their occupants from the real and the social. ‘Here in my car/I feel safest of all,’ sang Asperger’s sufferer Gary Numan in 1979’s ‘Cars’. ‘I can lock all my doors/It’s the only way to live.’ Is there a glimmer of hope at _Radio On_’s end when Robert abandons privatised transport (as favoured by Margaret Thatcher) for the shabby, inefficient but resolutely collective British Rail? Perhaps that’s to read too much into this resolutely oblique, brilliantly  opaque film.  
**Simon Reynolds, _Sight and Sound_, June 2008**
