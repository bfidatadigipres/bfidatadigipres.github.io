**‘I fell in love with Violette Nozière’ by Claude Chabrol**  
One night in 1933, a girl of 18 began a long odyssey through the Bois de Vincennes in a bus. This was Violette Nozière. Until this night she had lived in her parents’ tiny flat with Baptiste, her father, an engine-driver with the railways, and Germaine, her mother, a housewife. A thorough-going pair of inhibited petits bourgeois, the parents kept on day in and day out about their disappointments and about the hopes they entertained for their child. But Violette didn’t give a damn about all that: she wanted love, the grand passion, and she wanted lavish spending, gorgeous clothes, a life of bars, dance halls, sports cars, joyrides to the seaside. Her quest was for the ultimate.

But the ultimate was a little seedy-looking: a hotel in the Latin Quarter which she rented by the day with money stolen from her parents so as to be able to receive her friends, students more or less, who all more or less despised her. In the evenings, putting aside her black slashed dress and satin slippers, Violette returned in white ankle socks and pleated skirt to the family hearth where the little girl which she still and always remained in the wilfully blind eyes of her parents was awaited and expected to play pinochle. Violette could tell them nothing; they wouldn’t have understood, they would have killed her. So she played a double game: pathetic _femme fatale_ by day, reluctant child at night, unhappy child-woman night and day.

Unhappy because of her abortive dreams, unhappy because of this family which, turning morality to convention and hypocrisy to perversion, understood nothing. Perversity of a mother, herself an assurance of femininity, in repressing her daughter on principle as much as from any fear of possible competition; perversity of a father, holding Violette prisoner of his eyes and his insinuations.

When Violette contracted syphilis, she saw her parents under a new light as cowardly, contemptible, ludicrous. She began to hate them. Maybe she even thought then of killing them.

A catalytic element turned up. In the person of a handsome and hungry young man, unscrupulous, a mediocrity: Jean Dabin. He was the same age as Violette, claimed to be studying law and to be penniless, and he found her attractive. He needed a little helping hand financially.

Violette loved Dabin. With ardour, with passion. As utterly as it is possible to love. Ready to make any sacrifice, and glorying in her sacrifices. And Dabin let himself be loved; all the more willingly in that Violette did everything she could to provide him with the money he needed. Once she broke open the wardrobe at home in which Germaine kept her savings. Another time she blackmailed a wealthy public figure by accusing him, with letters as evidence, of being her father.

But Dabin’s demands became limitless, and the indifference with which he treated Violette grew more marked each day, forcing her always to promise more, edging her towards more permanent solutions.

Pending ‘her and her promises’, he left alone for Sables-d’Olonne. Violette could not break faith. She needed the 180,000 francs which her parents had scraped together day by day, and which she wanted to give to Dabin all at once as proof of her love. That day her parents had treated her abominably, searching through her things and forcing her to write a humiliating letter to the man she loved.

That evening, Violette poisoned her parents. Baptiste died. Germaine threw away half of her dose. She was to survive.

In the middle of the night, Violette returned home. The bodies of her parents were cluttering up the floor of the tiny apartment. She arranged things to look like a suicide by gas, and went to alert the neighbours. The Nozière case had begun.

Wandering around Paris, Violette gave the police the slip. She was no longer thinking of Dabin. It looks rather as though this crime had made her realise that her love was not returned – and perhaps realise, too, the futility of her act. Violette knew she was going to be arrested, that she had accomplished nothing, that no one had ever understood her, and that her only refuge now lay in prison. In the end she let the police catch up with her. She proceeded to confess. Yes, she meant to poison her father but not her mother, never.

She hated her father because he had raped her when she was 13 years old and continued to enjoy unnatural relations. Her testimony was recorded. She was confronted with her mother, who abused her and instigated a civil action against her.

Through this year of 1934, the year of the judicial enquiry and trial, Violette was alone in her prison, remembering. Yet the affair stirred up a great deal of interest. Among the champions of family life who were disposed to lynch her, among the majority who condemned her, and the handful of poets for whom she symbolised the courage to shatter (in Paul Eluard’s words) ‘the terrible serpents’ nest of blood relations,’ Violette rekindled an old story which transcended the one of which she was both author and victim, and which only time could efface from memory.

And it was in fact this road to oblivion that Violette was to take after her conviction; a road which was to make of her another woman who was finally pardoned.

The story as I tell it in the film stops with the end of the trial. Its theme is the mental confusion of an adolescent torn between the rigours of an outmoded education and the chaos of a reality where questions have no answers. Violette’s crime is the result of this tension between two poles.

Violette herself is a perfect illustration of the natural ambivalence of the human creature, which can flourish only in an environment that does not stifle it.

I fell in love with Violette Nozière long before I understood her. Others had fallen under the spell before me: Eluard, Aragon, Magritte, Simone de Beauvoir, Pierre Brasseur. In trying to bring her to life again, I felt the fascination of her ambiguities: murderer and saint, liar and true heart, childish yet inspired. I had to explore her mind, lay bare her dreams, suffer her daily existence. In this evocation of a headline sensation and a trial which had all France agog while Hitler was taking over control of Germany, it is no longer a question of judging, but of understanding.  
**_Monthly Film Bulletin_, April 1979 (This article appeared in a press brochure at the 1978 Cannes Film Festival; translation by Tom Milne)**
