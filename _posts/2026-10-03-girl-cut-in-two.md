**Claude Chabrol on ‘The Girl Cut in Two’**

‘A chaste film about perversion’ is Claude Chabrol’s description of his slyly amusing melodrama _The Girl Cut in Two_. His 56th feature tells the story of Gabrielle (Ludivine Sagnier), a television weathergirl who’s romantically torn between two contrasting men: novelist Charles (François Berléand), who’s several decades older than her, and volatile aristocratic playboy Paul (Benoît Magimel).

The film is inspired by a real-life crime of passion from 1906: the murder of US architect Stanford White, previously dramatised in Richard Fleischer’s 1955 film _The Girl in the Red Velvet Swing_, starring Joan Collins. _The Girl Cut in Two_ sees the source material transposed from US high society of the 1900s to contemporary Lyon, where nouveaux riches in publishing and television find themselves jostling for power with ‘old money’ puritans, embodied by Paul’s ruthless mother (Caroline Sihol), who scorns the lower-middle-class Gabrielle.

As ever with Chabrol, there’s a cunning playfulness at work. Hitchcockian doublings and dualities abound, narrative ellipses confuse our sense of time, and fairytale allusions – Gabrielle as Snow White looking for her Prince Charming – are plentiful. Illusory images prevail – right down to the closing act of magic.

_What was the starting point for_ The Girl Cut in Two _? Though it’s based on a true story, it has the feel of a dark fairytale._

I wanted to make a film about a young woman who appears on television, who is caught up in a semi-public existence, and who ends up leaving television by fabricating her disappearance. Then I remembered Richard Fleischer’s 1955 film _The Girl in the Red Velvet Swing_. I did some research [into the original case] and showed it to Cécile Maistre, who wrote the screenplay. I was struck by how this story could work in any country in any period. Transposing the story to France wasn’t a problem – a bourgeois family in France today is very similar to a family of that class in early 20th-century America; their way of thinking is very similar.

_Once again you’re drawn to the subject of power._

It’s not so much the negative impact power has on other people I’m interested in as the impact power has on oneself. I’m interested in the game of power, and the fact that people who think they have it don’t realise how ridiculous it is. A filmmaker thinks he or she has power, until the producer says that is the end, then the producer is thrown out by the company that employs him, and so forth. It’s an endless process. Power is limited to a certain field and to a certain period of time.

_How did you choose the cast?_

The real problem was choosing the young woman. I chose an actress who I liked and thought had talent, and who physically corresponded to my idea of the young woman. When I’m looking for an actor, I ask them to come and eat with me. I look at how they eat and drink. Ludivine [Sagnier] ate well and  drank well.

I know Benoît [Magimel] well. He has great potential – he’s not stuck in a particular type of leading-man part. A lot of actors limit themselves to young, good-looking roles, occasionally acting as a serial killer to break their image. That’s not the case with Benoît. The scriptwriter Cécile Maistre suggested François Berléand for the role of Charles. I wasn’t sure to what extent he’d be credible as a seducer of Ludivine. Then I thought of my friend Romain Gary, a French writer with a strange face, but a great womaniser. If he could seduce women, then so could Berléand!

_The theme of two men in competition recurs in your work from_ Le Beau Serge _on._

The idea of showing two different facets of men came very early in my filmmaking. I chose to portray two men because I understood men better than women then. With _Les Biches_ (1968) I swapped – instead of having two men, as in _Les Cousins_ (1959), I had two women. Not cousins but lesbians – but it came to the same thing. Women to me are more interesting than men.

_What interested you in the visual symbolism of the titular magic trick?_

I had the title before I came up with the closing scene, so I needed to justify it. I liked the symbolism of a woman being cut in half but staying intact – that to me is an advantage women have over men. A man cut in two, like Paul, becomes schizophrenic; a woman cut in two suffers, but then comes together again.

_An interesting aspect of_ The Girl Cut in Two _is that the aristocrats are the puritans and the nouveaux riches the libertines._

I know some aristocrats who are leftwing and some leftists who are puritans, but in my experience what you say is true. On the whole it is a chaste film about perversion – it had to be chaste otherwise it would have been a dirty film. Perversion is in one’s mind – that’s how you deal with it as a filmmaker, without showing it. I explained to Ludivine that she wouldn’t have to do anything uncomfortable: ‘There is only one thing – you’ll have to go on all fours with a feather stuck in your bum.’ And she said, ‘Oh, that’s all right then.’ I admired her when she said that.

**Interview and introduction by Thomas Dawson, _Sight and Sound_, June 2009**
