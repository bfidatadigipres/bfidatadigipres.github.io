Inspired by the true story of 17th-century priest Urbain Grandier, accused of bewitching a convent of nuns, _The Devils_ lays bare the collusion of church and state through a searing vision of religious persecution, collective hysteria and institutional corruption. Oliver Reed gives one of his greatest performances as Grandier, opposite an extraordinary Vanessa Redgrave as Sister Jeanne, whose repressed desire unleashes a religious and sexual mania. Derek Jarman’s visionary production design and Shirley Russell’s striking costumes help create a feverish world at once historical and startlingly modern. Censored from its original release, Russell’s film became one of cinema’s most legendarily compromised works. This long-anticipated restoration reaffirms _The Devils_ as an overwhelming, uncompromising and singular achievement of British cinema.  
**James Bell, BFI National Archive Senior Curator**

**Ken Russell on ‘The Devils’**  
At the time that I made _The_ _Devils_, I was a devout Catholic, and secure in my faith. I knew I wasn’t making a pornographic film. Far from it. In fact, the movie had the blessing of the Jesuits. Even to this day it is still taught at Loyola University in Chicago as a good Catholic movie. For myself, although I am not a political creature, I always viewed _The_ _Devils_ as my one political film. To me, it was about brainwashing, about the state taking over. It is based entirely upon John Whiting’s play _The_ _Devils_, and upon the writings of Aldous Huxley. Huxley was by no means a sensationalist – he was a historian of some note.

According to Huxley’s _The Devils of Loudun_, in 17th-century France there was a crisis caused by the desire of Cardinal Richelieu, the king and their cohorts to pull down the walls of the city of Loudun. This would render Loudun defenceless, so it wouldn’t be a threat to the crown or the church. The same had already happened to other cities, with the result that they subsequently had to concede to every indignity and injustice imposed upon them. The people of the town felt that as long as the walls of Loudun were standing, then they could defend themselves.

When I first read this story I was knocked out by it – it was just so shocking – and I wanted others to be knocked out by it too. I just felt I had to make it. I had done three films for United Artists, _Billion Dollar Brain_ [1967], _Women_ _in_ _Love_ and _The Music Lovers_, and they just automatically said: ‘Well, what do you want to do next?’ I said: ‘_The_ _Devils_,’ and they said: ‘Fine.’ Then somebody at UA actually read the script. The next thing, they refused to touch it. So Warner Bros picked it up.

I didn’t have the censors in mind when I shot the film, but I did have a great deal of respect for John Trevelyan. He was the chief censor of the BBFC at that time, and it was he who had been responsible for getting _Women in Love_ through the censors, complete with the nude male wrestling scene. I know he had to fight hard to convince the other members of the kangaroo court that this wrestling scene was Lawrence – it wasn’t just me making it up. It was an integral part of the story, and without it there was no meaning.

I don’t recall ever showing Trevelyan a script of _The_ _Devils_. As I remember, his first contact with the movie came when I actually showed him the cut that I wanted to call my own. Before that I really hadn’t discussed the picture with him at all. In the main, Trevelyan’s objections to my original cut were that the scenes of torture, such as the skewering of Grandier’s tongue with needles, or the breaking of his knees, simply went on too long. To him, these scenes needed only a few frames to tell the audience what was happening. After that, the sounds and their imaginations would fill in the rest. With the exception of one sequence, the Rape of Christ, which was removed in its entirety, all Trevelyan did was to tone down or reduce the scenes as I had cut them.

When the film was released in Britain, there were people who felt it was ‘disgusting’. My response was that I knew this was a disgusting event in the first place. I just filmed it. I stylised it, obviously, because I don’t believe in filming ancient cities as a load of mossy old rocks. To the people living in Loudun at that time, it was not ancient, it was a modern city. I know that the stylisation did throw some people, although many of the really weird things that they objected to, like the green lipstick, were completely factually accurate. In fact, we had to put a disclaimer at the beginning of the movie, one of those terrible, rolling, old-fashioned _Treasure_ _Island_ titles which said: ‘The events you are about to see actually happened.’

One thing I remember very clearly was hitting [the _Evening_ _Standards_ long-time critic] Alexander Walker with his own review of _The Devils_ because there were two instances to which he referred which were simply not in the film. One was that we see Oliver Reed’s penis being crushed. Well, that may be wishful thinking, but it simply is _not_ in the film. We were on a TV show [the BBC’s _Tonight_] together, and I confronted him about this inaccuracy, and he simply wouldn’t answer. In the end I got so exasperated that I just rolled up the paper and went _bamm_ [hitting Walker on the head with it] and then just walked off. We still smile at each other, occasionally, across crowded rooms.

When the film went to Venice, some high-ranking church official threatened to stop it being shown because he thought it was blasphemous. As a result, the people of Venice burnt him in effigy from a lamp post! Ha! And then there was the [conservative Christian campaign group] Nationwide Festival of Light picketing the film in Britain even though they had never seen it. I remember talking to these people outside a cinema and asking them why they hadn’t seen it. They were the usual morons, a slightly strange-looking lot, and of course it transpired that they were all just acting on instructions from above.   
**Ken Russell interviewed by Mark Kermode, originally published in the ‘Forbidden Cinema’ supplement to _Sight and Sound_ June 1995**
