Now that the Thirties Hollywood cinema is being constantly explored by television, we are in a much better position to assess the varied talents who were working at this time. But certain key ﬁlms have inevitably been overlooked, and one of the reasons for commenting on _Midnight_ is that it brought together a glittering array of star names on both sides of the camera. Produced by Arthur Hornblow Jr., directed by Mitchell Leisen and photographed by Charles Lang Jr, it also boasts a script by Billy Wilder and Charles Brackett which now seems a model of its kind – barbed, sly, irreverent and constructed in such a way that the story is always being propelled forward as one lie gives way to another.

The plot, in fact, draws on a variety of well-worn theatrical devices (as well as the whole Thirties comedy tradition), yet it is all made to seem extraordinarily fresh and vivid due mainly, I feel, to Messrs. Brackett and Wilder who were then at the peak of their collaboration, working for Lubitsch as well as later with Leisen again, on _Arise My Love_ and _Hold Back the Dawn_.

Hollywood has always been obsessed with the more ludicrous aspects of high society: European aristocracy with its penchant for easy living and easy spending crops up again and again in the Thirties – crumbling, lecherous princes and dukes living it up until the money runs out and made even more attractive by their apparent gentility and the aura of class that goes with it. Almost every character in _Midnight_ is involved in some kind of deception – even Eve, initially the most innocent, allows herself to be bought because she is penniless and ﬂattered by Jacques’ attentions and Flammarion’s material gifts. Brackett and Wilder capture this rapacious world in brittle, abrasive dialogue (‘It does something to your face – it gives you a chin’, says Eve to Hélène as the latter chooses a hat) and manage the many plot convolutions with practised ease and ﬂexibility.

A good example is the ballroom scene where Hélène is all ready to expose Eve as an impostor when ‘Baron Czerny’ turns up and makes a great show of being reunited with his loving wife. Having apparently been presented with evidence that they belong to her own class, Hélène accepts the situation with acid resignation, muttering ‘we nearly made a mistake’ to her companion as she gives Eve an obligatory smile in passing. As the narrative progresses, Eve is compelled to construct an ever-growing pinnacle of lies which reaches a high point of absurdity when she is forced to make an imaginary phone call to a non-existent ailing baby daughter in Budapest and Flammarion obligingly answers from a nearby extension (thus providing Barrymore with his greatest moment, vamping away to Eve: ‘It was a plain case of alcohol poisoning – we picked her up in the gutter’, and then imitating the baby’s voice for good measure). The scene is ﬁnally capped when, faced with Tibor’s determination to expose the whole masquerade, Eve persuades the others that her husband is subject to strange turns and obsessions (a sad heritage of the Czerny line!), like believing himself to be a taxi-driver. Again, class solidarity wins the day and they all band together to help Eve with her ‘family’ trouble.

One of the great qualities of _Midnight_ is its ability to point up these social mannerisms and attitudes without making a great issue of them – the ﬁlm never takes itself too seriously and never descends into vulgar parody. With the exception of Don Ameche (who is rather too pushing and heavy and totally non-European), the cast extracts every possible nuance from the writing: Mary Astor, supremely cool and bitchy; Barrymore (much less ﬂamboyant than usual and yet perfectly capturing Flammarion’s worldly-wise guile: ‘I don’t know what we’re falling into, but it’s not butter’); Francis Lederer, the ultimate in suave Thirties seducers and of course, Claudette Colbert, at the peak of her comedy form, slopping around in the rain in her evening dress at the beginning and giving a little secret smile when she thinks she has successfully crashed a posh reception.

If one attempts to analyse Mitchell Leisen’s contribution one is faced with that old Hollywood mystery: the direction seems virtually ‘invisible’, so what did he do? This of course, applies also to other major comedy talents of the period (Hawks and McCarey among them); all have differing preoccupations yet all are united by what may be termed respect for their material. They were fortunate in being surrounded by supreme technicians, writers who knew how to compose speakable dialogue and players who knew how to act. Leisen in _Midnight_ fuses all these qualities into a totally professional whole, without imposing a distinctive visual style of his own. Even so the overall timing, the judicious use of tracking shots to break up long dialogue scenes, the cuts to reaction shots at precisely the right moment all suggest the presence of a totally aware and sympathetic guiding hand.

Coming at the end of a decade, it is now possible to view _Midnight_ in the context of its times. Soon after, Hollywood comedy was to take a new turn with the arrival of Preston Sturges, the Wilder-Brackett partnership was drawing to a close with Wilder about to direct his own work, and the war was soon to initiate the ‘escapist’ comedy and the musical revue. Brackett, Wilder and Leisen brought to _Midnight_ a unique combination of urbanity and invective which has lost none of its sting. And surely Oscar Wilde himself would not be ashamed of Eve’s parting shot to Jacques before she goes off with her taxi-driver: ‘You must never get married, dear – it would be so unfortunate for so many women’.  
**John Gillett, _Focus on Film_, September/October 1970**
