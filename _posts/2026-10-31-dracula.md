_Dracula_ cemented Christopher Lee and Peter Cushing as one of horror cinema’s most iconic screen pairings, launched Hammer Films into the stratosphere and changed British horror and pop culture forever. In defiance of the censorship the film experienced on release, a new 4K restoration from Hammer reinstates footage unseen since 1958 outside of Japan. This special screening will include an extended introduction, with special guests celebrating the film and its restoration.  
**bfi.org.uk**

Hammer Films resurrected the horror film in 1957 with its spirited, colourful take on Mary Shelley’s foundational text (reimagining the major screen monster myth of an earlier generation). _The Curse of Frankenstein_ was directed by Terence Fisher, written by Jimmy Sangster and photographed by Jack Asher, with sets by Bernard Robinson, make-up by Phil Leakey and star turns by Peter Cushing and Christopher Lee. In 1931, Universal Pictures made _Frankenstein_ as a follow-up to their _Dracula_; Hammer reversed the order but furthered its gothic ascendancy by having the _Curse_ team deliver an even more startling adaptation of Bram Stoker’s vampire novel.

_Dracula_ (1958) – _Horror of Dracula_ in the US – surpassed _Curse_ in every way and inspired even more sequels, spinoffs and imitations, with a rerelease in UK cinemas and a definitive 4K Blu-ray box-set out this month. Soon after his star-making appearance as the Count, Lee was in Italy parodying himself in a comedy, _Tempi duri per I vampiri_ (_Uncle Was a Vampire_, 1959). Within months of that, Cushing’s Van Helsing was tackling a new arch-nemesis in _The Brides of Dracula_ (1960).

Sangster’s script for _Dracula_ cuts down Stoker’s densely populated, continent-spanning plot to something more contained. Action takes place either side of a border between Klausenburg (Cluj, in Romania) and Carlstadt (a district of Düsseldorf, Germany). This Middle Europe is an imaginary territory populated by fussy British character actors who heft steins but mostly conduct themselves as if they were in a Dickens film. Indeed, the improvised finale in which Van Helsing pulls down the curtains in Castle Dracula to let in the sunlight and dispel the Count is very like the finish of David Lean’s _Great Expectations_ (1946), in which the shrinking ‘monster’ is Martita Hunt, who would show up in _The Brides of Dracula_. Sangster repeats the trick of building a story around a Cushing character’s often-frustrated plan, which worked so well in _Curse_, where the Baron wants to create a perfect creature but lesser people hinder and compromise his vision. Top-billed Cushing is the star of _Dracula_ and the film is about Van Helsing’s determination to end a monster’s reign of terror, despite the unreliability of everyone he enlists in his crusade. In most versions of _Dracula_, the plot motor is the bloodlust which leads Lee to leave the castle in search of fresh territory. Here, Lee’s Count is someone who wants his library catalogued. Jonathan Harker (John Van Eyssen) comes to Castle Dracula as Van Helsing’s agent, plotting to assassinate the vampire (though he’s ill-prepared and swiftly blunders to his death).

Dracula’s actions in the remainder of the film are motivated by revenge, not due to the temerity of Van Helsing, but because of Harker’s staking of his vampire companion (Valerie Gaunt). He goes after Harker’s fiancée Lucy (Carol Marsh) and sister-in-law Mina (Melissa Stribling) as replacements. The approach puts the Count on the back foot as a villain: undoubtedly evil, he’s also someone whose hospitality is sorely abused by an employee out to kill him. Lee’s imposing, cloaked vampire is an affront to Victorian morality – he switches from polite if chilly aristocrat to feral, snarling, bloody-fanged beast, and his merest glance is enough to enslave women. Once bitten, Lucy and Mina become sly, duplicitous and eager to be exsanguinated. They collaborate with their new master and make fools of family and friends who try to help. Van Helsing is only warm when examining the little girl (Janina Faye) Lucy has tried to lure into the bushes. Otherwise, his rudeness and impatience with lesser mortals inhibit his effectiveness as a crusader. His bluster fails to impress a border official (George Benson), who is then bribed by the less morally inflexible Arthur (Michael Gough) to provide vital clues.

In a 2013 _Sight and Sound_ Blu-ray review, Philip Kemp noted, ‘The blood, and Dracula’s final bodily dissolution, evidently exercised the BBFC more than the film’s eroticism, and several cuts were demanded. It’s this shortened version that’s been released on VHS and DVD until now, with muddy colour and in the wrong ratio. But an uncut print was preserved in a Japanese archive. It was badly damaged in a fire, but the last four reels survived, rather battered. Thanks to valiant restoration work by the BFI, Molinare and Deluxe 142, excised scenes have been cleaned up and seamlessly reinserted, the colour regraded and the correct 1.66:1 ratio restored.’

A certain amount of legend-printing was involved, as Hammer’s Steve Rogers says: ‘There was only ever one version of _Dracula_ filmed – the one Terence Fisher made for Hammer. It was subsequently censored by the BBFC in the UK, and slightly less censored in America. In Japan, and apparently some parts of Europe, it was shown uncensored, which is why it’s become known to posterity as “The Japanese Version”, even though it’s not – a situation muddied when producer Tony Hinds told a film magazine in 1958 that they were creating an extended version with more blood in it just for Japan. They weren’t. It’s just that the Japanese weren’t expected to censor the original film. Different countries censored films in different ways but, seemingly, Japan had a higher tolerance for such things. This is why, when a fan called Simon Rowson doggedly tracked down an incomplete, water-and-fire-damaged copy of “The Japanese Version” in 2011, great rejoicing was witnessed worldwide and elements of that damaged print were edited into the then-current _Dracula_ restoration. Everyone believed that was as good as it would ever get. When Hammer and The Warner Archive Collection partnered up, it was known that Warners held the best materials for restoration – the negatives – but they had recently discovered a can marked up as “Japanese inserts”. On examination it was discovered that not only did this can include pristine versions of the censored shots that had previously been recovered in poor condition, but there was also an additional shot not in the Japanese print. Maybe _Dracula_ was censored in Japan after all?’

Two key sequences – Dracula’s seduction of Mina and his later disintegration – are vastly more effective in this new big-screen and Blu-ray version, though the restored footage amounts to shots of a few seconds in length. As vital is the work done to finesse the image, presenting Jack Asher’s cinematography with a clarity, a richness and a detail that proves a revelation. Note the skulls in Dracula’s crypt, the pea-green wallpaper in the house of sickness and the clutter in the library. In addition, we now can see from the piles of random volumes strewn about that, indeed, the Count does badly need someone to catalogue and reshelve his book collection.  
**Kim Newman, _Sight and Sound_, November 2026**
