**SPOILER WARNING** The following notes give away some of the plot.

_The Crying Game_, an unusual story of an IRA operative who seeks out and falls for the ‘widow’ of a British squaddie he helped to kidnap remains Jordan’s best known and most widely celebrated work, and won him an Oscar for Best Original Screenplay. It is therefore ironic to note that the film almost wasn’t made. No backers could be found for it in either the United States or Ireland because the plot, which involved an interracial love story between a Black English transsexual and an Irish republican gunman, was considered far too taboo. British production companies were equally reluctant to take the film unless serious changes were made. Jordan, however, had been working on this scenario, which he originally called _The Soldier’s Wife_ (it was Stanley Kubrick who suggested the title change, warning Jordan that audiences would otherwise mistake it for a war movie), since he completed _Angel_ [1982] and he was loath to change anything now that he had developed it to his satisfaction.

Stephen Woolley, the former head of Palace Pictures and Jordan’s long-time producer, finally patched together a £2.3m budget from four sources: British Screen; Channel 4; Eurotrustees; and a Japanese distribution company, Nippon Development & Finance. The money was, however, barely adequate for a film of this scope and shooting began before all the finance was in place. Palace Pictures was also close to folding (and would do so before _The Crying Game_ was released); on the third day of filming, the production was almost shut down and the original cinematographer quit. In order to keep the wheels moving, Woolley had to resort to using his own credit cards and raiding the tills at his London cinema, The Scala. As tense as the shoot was, however, the cast and crew soldiered on as there was a strong sense that the film was going to be something special.

_The Crying Game_ was not an overnight success. On the contrary, it was the very definition of a sleeper hit. The film was refused entry at Cannes and was released in Ireland and the UK with little fanfare. The early notices in Ireland were positive, but the British reviews were mixed and in a case of very bad timing, the release coincided with a new IRA offensive in England. Most discussions of the film therefore centred on its politics, which nobody seemed able to agree upon (the film was alternatively accused of being pro- and anti-IRA). Moreover, critics were uncertain of how to review a movie that seemed to rely so heavily on a twist revealed halfway through the narrative. The film seemed destined to cause a brief stir, as all films about the IRA do, and then fade into relative obscurity. Woolley’s company Palace was wound down after going into receivership, while Jordan returned to writing fiction and published his third novel, _Sunrise with Sea Monster_ (1994), as he was certain his film career was over. The US release changed all of that. Miramax, which was then a fledgling company, had refused to help fund _The Crying Game_ but quickly agreed to distribute it after Harvey Weinstein saw the finished version. In what seems like a gimmick more suited to a schlocky 1950s thriller, the Miramax advertising campaign asked both critics and viewers not to reveal the key plot twist. The US critics were less concerned with politics and played along, and audiences seemed willing to overlook Jordan’s use of cricketing metaphors (usually the kiss of death for a British film in America).

The film soon began generating considerable word of mouth. It was moved from art-house theatres into mainstream cinemas and as many as 1,000 prints were finally in circulation. Miramax also began campaigning for the film to be considered at the 1993 Academy Awards. It was eventually nominated in six categories, including Best Picture for Woolley, Best Director and Best Original Screenplay for Jordan, Best Actor for Stephen Rea, Best Supporting Actor for Jaye Davidson (a nomination that gave away the film’s twist by revealing the actor playing Dil to be a man) and Best Editing for Kant Pan. Although only Jordan’s screenplay finally won, the nominations further increased interest in the film, which went on to gross more than $60m and, in the process, helped establish Miramax as a major player in US film and revived the fortunes of both Jordan and Woolley.

Jordan richly deserved his Oscar for _The Crying Game_. His screenplay offers a masterclass in structure and narrative construction. Although some critics complained that the film is divided into two distinct halves (some also said of variable quality), it is entirely of a piece. Indeed, everything that happens in the second half of the film, in which Fergus arrives in London to find Dil, is set up during the first half of the film, in which Fergus kidnaps and befriends Jody in Ireland. From the opening moments, during which we hear Percy Sledge sing ‘When a Man Loves a Woman’, and from the first lines of dialogue (Jody saying: ‘Now that’s cricket, hon,’ or ‘Never pissed holding a girl’s hand, Jude.’), Jordan established a tone of irony and double meaning. It is so subtly done, however, that it is only upon subsequent viewings that his sly game becomes apparent.

Of course, it is not the screenplay alone that has made the film a modern classic. Credit must also go to the cast. Jordan’s films have generally been very well acted but the ensemble in _The Crying Game_ is almost note perfect. Rea, Davidson and Miranda Richardson could not be better, Forest Whitaker’s screen presence and intelligence more than make up for his wavering accent and smaller roles by Adrian Dunbar, Ralph Brown and Jim Broadbent have just the right amount of menace and ambiguity. Credit must also go to Jordan’s direction; the film’s noirish visuals, courtesy of replacement cinematographer Ian Wilson; and Kant Pan’s tight editing. _The Crying Game_ is a masterpiece and, like all masterpieces, it is a work you can revisit in the knowledge that you will find something new each time – even if you already know the twist.  
**Brian Hoyle, extract from _The Crying Game_ DVD/Blu-ray booklet essay (BFI, 2017)**
